# Two-hop pencil graph for genus 5

Repository proof status is unverified. [pcc:unverified]

## Bound and hypotheses

Let \(C/\mathbb F_q\) be a smooth, projective, geometrically integral non-hyperelliptic curve of genus 5 in characteristic greater than 4. Assume that \(W^1_4(C)\) is a geometrically integral curve, \(C_{\overline{\mathbb F}_q}\) has no \(g^1_3\) or \(g^2_4\), and at least one geometric degree-four pencil is simply branched. Here simply branched means one ramification point of index two over each branch value. Then, with a bounded-degree birational plane model and the Jacobian order supplied, the discrete logarithm problem in \(\operatorname{Jac}(C)(\mathbb F_q)\) has expected bit complexity \(\widetilde O(q^{4/3})\) by the construction below. The asymptotic constants may be taken along extensions of a fixed such curve. For a varying family the same argument is uniform with bounded presentations of the pencil locus and its non-simple-branching locus.

The exponent was previously proposed heuristically. This argument supplies the shallow large-prime tree under explicit geometric hypotheses. The edge events are dependent, and the statement is asymptotic. The degree-four pencil locus is one-dimensional under the genus-5 Brill–Noether–Petri generality assumptions in characteristic zero. The finite-field statement uses the explicit hypotheses above. [Diem–Kochinke, §4.2.2](https://doczz.net/doc/7777497/computing-discrete-logarithms-with-special-linear-systems) · [Lazarsfeld, Brill–Noether–Petri without degenerations](https://www.math.stonybrook.edu/~roblaz/Reprints/Lazarsfeld.BNP.pdf)

## The graph before sampling

The two vertex sets are \(\mathbb F_q\)-rational simply branched pencils \(L\) of degree four and rational points \(P\) of \(C\). Since \(\operatorname{Br}(\mathbb F_q)=0\), a rational class \(L\in W^1_4(C)(\mathbb F_q)\) is represented by a line bundle over \(\mathbb F_q\). The absence of \(g^1_3\) and \(g^2_4\) makes every such pencil base-point-free and complete of projective dimension one. There are \(q+O(\sqrt q)\) vertices on each side by the Weil bound, applying it to the normalized pencil curve and excluding a finite subset of non-simply-branched pencils. The host edge \(L\mathbin{-}P\) exists when the fiber of \(L\) through \(P\) consists of four distinct rational points. Its support is the set of the three other points in that fiber.

**Bounded pair incidence.** Fix two distinct geometric simply branched pencils. A simply branched degree-four map has geometric monodromy \(S_4\). The inertia transpositions generate the geometric group because \(\mathbb P^1\) has no nontrivial connected étale cover, and a transitive group generated by transpositions is \(S_4\). The point stabilizer \(S_3\) is maximal, so the degree-four map has no proper intermediate cover. Consequently the two distinct pencils give a birational map to their image of bidegree \((4,4)\) in \(\mathbb P^1\times\mathbb P^1\). Only a bounded number of unordered point pairs have coincident images for both maps, as such pairs lie over singularities of the bounded-degree image. Remove the endpoints of these pairs when defining the proof host and discard every fiber containing an endpoint. Each pencil loses at most a bounded number of host edges. For every remaining pair \(Q,R\), the condition that they lie in one fiber is a proper closed condition on the integral curve \(W^1_4(C)\). The condition is a rank drop of the evaluation of sections of \(L\) at \(Q,R\). Its zero-dimensional locus has uniformly bounded size from the determinantal system on four canonical points described below. Thus a support pair occurs in \(O(1)\) host edges. For a fixed output point \(P\) and a candidate support point \(Q\), at most \(O(1)\) host edges with output \(P\) contain \(Q\) in their support. At a fixed pencil, a support point belongs to at most three edges, since it belongs to one fiber.

**Host mixing.** For each simply branched \(L\), the Galois closure \(X_L\) of the map to \(\mathbb P^1\), viewed over \(C\), is an \(S_3\)-Galois cover. Its geometric branch locus on \(C\) is the set of the two unramified points above each of the 16 simple branch values. Inertia there is a transposition in \(S_3\). The ramified point of \(C\) above a simple branch value need not ramify in \(X_L\to C\), so when testing complete splitting it must also be excluded. This omits at most 16 points per pencil. Away from those fibers, an \(\mathbb F_q\)-point \(P\) is completely split in \(X_L\to C\) exactly when the degree-four fiber through \(P\) consists of four distinct rational points. The splitting density is \(1/6\), since the arithmetic \(S_4\) group contains the full geometric \(S_4\). The \(S_3\) cover over \(C\) is therefore regular too. The row degree is \(q/6+O(\sqrt q)\), by applying Weil to \(X_L\) and dividing the unramified rational-point count by 6.

If \(X_L\) and \(X_M\) have different branch loci on \(C\), their geometric function fields are linearly disjoint over the function field of \(C\). To see this, a branch point belonging to just one locus would kill a transposition in the common Galois quotient. One transposition normally generates \(S_3\), so the quotient is trivial. Their compositum is then regular with group \(S_3\times S_3\), and has bounded genus by tame Riemann–Hurwitz. The number of common neighbors of \(L,M\) is \(q/36+O(\sqrt q)\). For any branch locus there are only a bounded number of possible \(S_3\) covers of \(C\) over the algebraic closure. The prime-to-characteristic tame fundamental group of a genus-5 curve punctured at 32 points has a bounded number of generators. Each such closure has genus 73 and has a uniformly bounded automorphism group. A choice of \(S_4\) subgroup of this automorphism group extending the \(S_3\) action determines the quotient map defining a pencil, up to change of target coordinate. It follows that each pencil has only \(O(1)\) exceptional other pencils with the very same geometric branch locus. [SGA 1, Exposé XIII, Corollary 2.12](https://jcreinhold.github.io/sga/i/13-cohomological-properness.html)

Write \(A\) for the pencil-by-point adjacency matrix and \(E=A-\frac16J\) for its centered form, where \(J\) is the rectangular all-ones matrix. Deleting the bounded exceptional fibers in each row preserves the above estimates. The diagonal of \(EE^{\mathsf T}\) and the bounded number of exceptional entries per row are \(O(q)\). Other entries are \(O(\sqrt q)\), by the row degrees, codegrees and number of point vertices. Hence its maximal absolute row sum is \(O(q^{3/2})\), and \(\lVert E\rVert=O(q^{3/4})\). The squared deviation of the point degrees from one-sixth of the number of pencils is at most \(\lVert E\rVert^2 O(q)=O(q^{5/2})\). Discard \(O(\sqrt q)\) marked point vertices whose host degree is less than a sufficiently small constant times \(q\). Pencil degrees stay \(\Omega(q)\) and the mixing estimate persists by taking a column submatrix. These latter marked points may still be used in supports. Both sides of the trimmed host have minimum degree \(\Omega(q)\).

## First sample gives expansion

Use three independent Bernoulli factor bases \(B_1,B_2,B_3\). Each includes each rational point independently with probability \(p=q^{-1/3}(\log q)^4\), for sufficiently large \(q\). An equivalent dyadic probability within a fixed factor of \(p\) uses only \(O(\log q)\) random bits per sample decision. Let \(k_0=\lfloor q^{2/3}(\log q)^2\rfloor\). In graph \(G_i\) an edge of the host is retained when its entire support is in \(B_i\). The marked output point may itself belong to \(B_i\). The graph label remains valid in that case.

Fix a \(k\)-set \(S\), \(1\le k\le k_0\), on either side and a set \(T\) of at most \(2k\) vertices on the other side. There are \(m=\Omega(kq)\) candidate edges from \(S\) to the complement of \(T\). A specified support point occurs in \(O(k)\) candidate edges by the fixed-pencil and fixed-output bounds, depending on the side of \(S\). Any specified support pair occurs in \(O(1)\) edges. Thus the sum of pairwise joint probabilities over intersecting support tests is

\[
\Delta=O(mkp^5+mp^3),\qquad \mu=mp^3.
\]

Pairs sharing one support bit contribute \(p^5\). Pairs sharing at least two contribute at most \(p^3\). Duplicate three-point supports have bounded multiplicity, so one may keep one representative of each support, changing \(m\) by a constant factor in this calculation. Janson’s inequality bounds the probability of no retained edge by \(\exp[-\Omega(\min(kqp^3,qp))]\). For all \(k\le k_0\), the minimum grows faster than \(k\log q\) by a uniform factor diverging with \(q\). A union bound over \(S,T\) therefore gives \(|N_{G_1}(S)|>2|S|\) for every such \(S\), with probability \(1-o(1)\). Every vertex consequently reaches at least \(k_0\) pencil vertices within \(O(\log q)\) steps in \(G_1\). [Janson’s inequality](https://doi.org/10.1002/rsa.3240010209) [pcc:unverified]

## Two further samples join the expanded neighborhoods

Condition on \(G_1\). For each vertex fix \(k_0\) reachable pencil vertices within the above distance. If two chosen pencil sets \(S,T\) meet, the corresponding neighborhoods already join. Otherwise consider two-hop paths \(L\mathbin{-}P\mathbin{-}M\) from \(S\) to \(T\). The spectral bound gives

\[
\lVert E^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol 1_S\rVert_2^2=O(q^{3/2}k_0).
\]

It follows that all but \(O(q^{3/2}/k_0)=o(q)\) point vertices have \(\Omega(k_0)\) neighbors in \(S\). The same holds for \(T\). Thus the host has \(m=\Omega(qk_0^2)\) candidate two-hop paths between the chosen sets. Test the support of the first edge in \(B_2\) and that of the second edge in \(B_3\). A single path uses six Bernoulli bits, three from each sample. A physical point that appears in both supports contributes two independent bits.

To bound overlaps between paths, fix one candidate path and prescribe which of its bits a second path must share.

| Shared bits | Number of eligible second paths per choice of bits | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One | \(O(k_0^2)\) | There are \(O(k_0)\) edges on the specified side using that support point, and each can extend in \(O(k_0)\) ways. |
| Two or more | \(O(k_0)\) | Two bits from the same sample fix that side's edge up to \(O(1)\) choices. For one from each sample, the first bit gives \(O(k_0)\) first edges, and the resulting marked point and the second bit limit the other side to \(O(1)\) edges. |
| Four or more | \(O(1)\) | At least two bits are from one sample and at least one from the other. Fixing the first side up to \(O(1)\) choices also fixes the marked point. The bit from the other side then leaves at most \(O(1)\) choices. |

These bounds also give \(O(1)\) multiplicity for identical six-bit supports. Count one representative for each support. Splitting the overlap sum by one, two to three, and at least four shared bits gives

\[
\Delta=O\!\left(m(k_0^2p^{11}+k_0p^9+p^6)\right),\qquad \mu=mp^6.
\]

Janson’s inequality bounds failure to find a path by \(\exp[-\Omega(\min(qp,qk_0p^3,qk_0^2p^6))]=\exp[-\Omega(q^{1/3})]\). There are only \(O(q^2)\) pairs of fixed neighborhoods after conditioning on \(G_1\). A union bound joins them all with probability \(1-o(1)\). Thus the union graph has a single component containing every trimmed-host vertex and with diameter \(O(\log q)\).

## Constructing the graph and solving the logarithm

The algorithm constructs the sampled subgraph directly. It enumerates \(C(\mathbb F_q)\) in expected \(\widetilde O(q)\) via a bounded-degree map to \(\mathbb P^1\), samples the three bases, and lets \(F=B_1\cup B_2\cup B_3\). With high probability \(F\) has size \(\widetilde O(q^{2/3})\). Each retained edge has three distinct rational support points in one sample. Therefore at least one pair \(Q,R\) in \(F\) can be used to find it. For each distinct pair in \(F\), enumerate the ordered distinct rational residual pairs \(U,V\) on \(C\) satisfying

\[
h^0(Q+R+U+V)\ge2.
\]

In the canonical model in \(\mathbb P^4\), this is rank at most 3 for the matrix of the four evaluation columns. The system can be solved on finitely many fixed projective charts with diagonals removed. The equations have bounded degrees in a fixed number of variables. Use Gröbner elimination for the zero-dimensional cases and bounded-degree finite-field factorization to recover rational solutions. Skip a pair if its system has positive dimension. The proof host discarded all endpoints of exceptionally mobile pairs, so every required edge is still returned by a zero-dimensional query. Each pencil class can be keyed by the reduced representative of the effective fiber minus \(4P_*\) for a fixed rational point \(P_*\). Non-simply-branched pencils need not be recognized for the algorithm, because any returned distinct rational divisor with \(h^0=2\) gives a valid pencil relation. Retain the edge for any choice of marked point whose other three points belong to one of the samples. Extra valid edges preserve the paths from the trimmed host. There are \(\widetilde O(q^{4/3})\) pair queries and their expected cost is polynomial in \(\log q\) apiece. [Diem–Kochinke, §4.2.2](https://doczz.net/doc/7777497/computing-discrete-logarithms-with-special-linear-systems) [pcc:unverified]

Use breadth-first search to reach enough point vertices along paths of length at most \(O((\log q)^2)\), resampling if the base is too large or the coverage test fails. Randomly choose a root point, which has probability \(1-o(1)\) of belonging to the trimmed host. Add it to \(F\), after sampling, if necessary. In each edge relation,

\[
[P]-[L]=-\sum_{U\text{ in the support}}[U].
\]

Subtract the two relations meeting at each pencil on a root path. This eliminates the degree-four class with coefficients \(+1\) and \(-1\), and expresses \([P]\) as the root class plus a signed sum of \(O((\log q)^2)\) classes from \(F\). Make a depth-one rooted tree of these point expressions for the covered points outside \(F\). Each edge is a one-large-prime label in the required form \([P]+\sum_{U\in F}r_U[U]=0\), with no extra pencil-class columns. It is important to use these direct expressions because the pencil cancellation between two point variables gives opposite signs, while Diem's original two-large-prime edge definition uses two positive signs.

With \(g=5,c=3\), Diem’s Proposition 11 requires a factor base of size \(\widetilde O(q^{2/3})\), this sort of shallow labeled tree, and at least \(q^{1-1/5+1/15}=q^{13/15}\) points in the union of the factor base and tree vertices. The graph covers \(q-O(\sqrt q)\) point vertices. Proposition 11 then finishes the discrete logarithm in expected \(\widetilde O(q^{4/3})\). Tree construction and extraction stay within the same bound, including restarts. Proposition 11 supplies the final relation and linear algebra guarantee. [Diem, Proposition 11](https://jtnb.centre-mersenne.org/item/10.5802/jtnb.815.pdf)
